## Fourth GEO Public Lecture

This is the fourth GEO public lecture of the year.

The topic this time is *Official Website GEO*, inspired by an increasingly clear trend: AI platforms are placing greater emphasis on official websites as authoritative sources.

Overseas models have long cited official websites extensively; now, major domestic AI platforms are moving in the same direction.

In the AI era, official websites—beyond their traditional roles in branding and customer acquisition—are gaining growing influence and weight in how AI systems operate.

![image.png](图片和附件/image%204.png)


Key takeaways:

1. Official website GEO simultaneously affects brand visibility and decision-making conversion.  
2. Official websites will become one of the most critical *sources of fact* for enterprises in the AI era. They host first-hand, enterprise-owned facts—and at the GEO level, they provide AI with traceable, standardized answers.  
3. A strong official website GEO system hinges on achieving three core goals: *being visible to AI*, *being trusted by AI*, and *being citable by AI*.  
4. Enterprise facts are raw material; user questions determine what gets produced; web pages deliver it; and technology + data ensure content is discoverable, understandable, and continuously testable.  
5. The knowledge base is the foundational content layer of official website GEO—and the most labor-intensive part of the entire project.  
6. AI can organize facts, but enterprises must define and verify them.  
7. Accuracy demands on official website content are high: knowledge base quality directly shapes the accuracy, richness, and repetition rate of downstream AI-generated output.  
8. The knowledge base answers *what the enterprise knows*; question engineering answers *what users want to know*.  
9. The title library’s value lies in transforming real user questions into sustainable, operational content tasks.  
10. Early-stage official website operations can begin with 40 high-value questions—prioritizing those most frequently asked, closest to purchase decisions, and best supported by enterprise evidence.  
11. Page writing should follow four principles: clear conclusions, scannable structure, key claims backed by evidence, and explicit scope boundaries plus next steps.  
12. Content generation can be automated—but factual standards and publishing responsibility must remain human-led. Automation increases update frequency, yet enterprises must still own and rigorously manage the knowledge base, quality assurance, and ongoing validation.  
13. On the technical side, an official website GEO system can be audited across four stages: *discovery*, *acquisition*, *understanding*, and *measurement*.  
14. Tools are just execution vehicles. What truly determines long-term outcomes is whether enterprise facts are complete, whether user questions are accurately covered, and whether page-level evidence is solid.  
15. Ultimately, the official website must accomplish one thing: ensure the enterprise’s authentic information is *seen*, *understood*, and *accurately cited* by AI.

## GEOFlow 3.0

AI citations of corporate websites are increasing—and with them, the value of website-based GEO.

Over the past two weeks, we consumed over 5 billion tokens to complete this major iteration: GEOFlow 3.0. It’s now officially open-sourced.

I define GEOFlow 3.0 as: *the world’s first open-source GEO intelligent operations system built specifically for corporate websites and authoritative source sites.*

This release restructures the backend, AI workspace, article quality assurance, distribution pipeline, and upgrade workflow—giving GEOFlow 3.0 a more complete, production-ready product shape.

GEOFlow’s core use case is the corporate website. Companies can consolidate product documentation, customer case studies, FAQs, industry knowledge, and brand guidelines into a unified knowledge base—and then connect content generation, AI quality checks, human review, website publishing, cross-channel distribution, and analytics into one seamless chain.

The same content engineering framework also supports GEO sub-channels, standalone source sites, internal content management dashboards, and multi-site operations.

We’ve especially strengthened four capabilities essential for long-term, sustainable GEO operations on corporate websites:

1. **Admin UI upgraded to V3**  
   All major pages now share consistent navigation (sidebar + top bar), form layouts, and interaction patterns. New features include “recently visited” tracking, adjustable sidebar width, responsive mobile layout, keyboard navigation support, and full accessibility compliance—including multilingual interface support.

2. **AI Quality Assurance integrated into the publishing gate**  
   Every article is evaluated against task-specific knowledge, versioned advertising policies, and contextual publishing requirements. The system checks for knowledge consistency, data accuracy & citation integrity, advertising compliance risks, and overall content completeness. For each check, it returns granular scores, exact locations in the source text, supporting evidence, regulatory references, concrete revision suggestions, and full audit history.

3. **Multi-site distribution + human publishing collaboration—now a closed loop**  
   Hosted channel sites support subdomain assignment, per-article routing, publishing quotas, failure cooldown periods, technical pre-checks, cache invalidation, and status reconciliation. GEOFlow Agent, WordPress REST API, generic HTTP API, and target-site packages remain fully supported.  
   The human publishing workspace now integrates the Chrome Operations Assistant: it pairs with your device, issues work orders via minimal-scope tokens, verifies login credentials, auto-fills drafts, and returns execution receipts. Final publication always requires explicit confirmation by an operator—and the entire workspace can be installed as a PWA for standalone use.

4. **Clearer engineering boundaries for API, CLI, updates, and recovery**  
   API v1 and GEOFlow CLI cover directories, tasks, execution logs, assets, articles, and browser-based operational protocols. A dedicated GEOFlow Updater handles site upgrades, full backups, environment validation, and point-in-time rollback. High-risk operations require both administrator password + six-digit authenticator code.

For a corporate website to earn sustained AI citations and recommendations, it needs *real knowledge*, *stable content*, *clear structure*, and *consistent operations*. Models boost efficiency—but only when paired with a robust knowledge base, rigorous quality gates, disciplined publishing workflows, scalable site-and-content engineering, and actionable data feedback can that efficiency translate into lasting results.

## Managing Overhead Costs

![Codex Image 2026-08-30 11:08:39.png](图片和附件/Codex%20图像%202026年8月30日%2011_08_39.png)

For early-stage startups, one of the most important operational principles is to minimize management overhead.

Here, “management overhead” is used in a broad sense. It includes:

1. Salaries and time spent by managers  
2. Salaries and time spent by HR, administrative, finance, and project management staff  
3. Time consumed by meetings, status reports, approvals, cross-team coordination, performance reviews, and internal information flow  

These costs share one key trait: they sustain the organization itself—but do *not* directly create customer value.

For mature, large companies, some level of management overhead is necessary. As scale and complexity grow, formal rules and mechanisms become essential.

Startups are different.

At an early stage, a startup’s truly scarce resources boil down to just three things: money, people, and time.

Imagine a 20-person company where five people primarily handle management, coordination, reporting, and support. That means 25% of the team’s capacity isn’t directly applied to product development, sales, or delivery—making it extremely costly for an early-stage business.

So I believe startups should consciously track a simple metric:  
**Broadly defined management overhead ÷ total operating cost**

For early-stage startups, I’d aim to keep this ratio at or below 20%—a sign of relative health.

Another useful metric is:  
**Frontline value creators ÷ total headcount**

“Frontline value creators” are those directly involved in product work, engineering, sales, and delivery—including their associated costs. For early-stage startups, this ratio should ideally exceed 80%.

Put simply: the earlier the stage, the more people should be *close to the product*, *close to the customer*, and *close to revenue*.

You can even adopt a straightforward heuristic:  
→ The closer to the customer, the more people you need there.  
→ The farther from the customer, the fewer people you need there.

Many startups fall into a trap as they grow: when business issues arise, they add management roles; when information flows break down, they layer on more reporting; when execution lags, they introduce more approvals. Over time, the organization becomes “more complete”—but also heavier and more expensive.

Good management doesn’t mean adding more control points. It means enabling *more people to create more value with fewer management interventions*.

When goals are clear, responsibilities are assigned, standards are explicit, data is transparent, and feedback is timely—then much of the meeting clutter, redundant reporting, and middle-layer coordination becomes unnecessary.

This shift is becoming even more pronounced in the AI era.

Tasks once requiring dedicated roles—statistical analysis, report generation, information synthesis, project tracking, knowledge sharing, process reminders—are increasingly automated by systems and AI agents.

That means startup organizations can—and should—become lighter.

A small group of high-caliber individuals, amplified by robust AI capabilities and supported by minimal, well-designed management structures, can now accomplish what previously required dozens—or even hundreds—of people.

So the core of startup management is this:  
→ *Fewer management actions*, but *higher-quality ones*.  
→ *Lighter organizational structure*, but *higher density of value creation*.

## The Kelly Criterion

![Codex Image 2026-08-30 11_15_25.png](图片和附件/Codex%20图像%202026年8月30日%2011_15_25.png)

The Kelly Criterion is a formula that calculates the optimal fraction of your total capital to wager on each bet—based on the probability of winning or losing. First published by John Larry Kelly Jr. in 1956 in the *Bell System Technical Journal*, it tells you how much to stake per round when odds and probabilities are known.

![image.png](图片和附件/image%205.png)

At its core, the Kelly Criterion answers this question: Given an uncertain opportunity with a win probability, a payoff if you win, and a loss if you lose—how much capital (or resource) should you commit to maximize long-term compound growth while minimizing the risk of ruin?

It applies far beyond gambling—to investing, forecasting, entrepreneurship, marketing budgeting, and even personal attention allocation:

1. Investing and trading  
2. Gambling and sports prediction—the original classic use case, where repeated bets have explicit odds  
3. Startup ventures and product experiments—many entrepreneurial decisions are, at heart, resource bets  
4. Marketing budget allocation—not just historical ROI, but also win probability, payoff multiple, data reliability, and worst-case loss  
5. Scientific research and R&D projects  
6. Personal time and attention allocation—our time is finite; every activity we choose carries a success probability, potential upside, and opportunity cost  

The Kelly Criterion centers on two practical questions:  
- Is this opportunity worth acting on at all?  
- If yes, what proportion of available resources should I allocate?

Beneath those two questions lie three foundational ideas:

1. A high expected value does *not* justify heavy exposure.  
2. Overcommitting usually hurts more than undercommitting slightly.  
3. Its goal is sustained compound growth—not short-term gains.

In plain terms: When you’re confident, go bigger—but never go all-in. When you’re uncertain, scale back. Even the best opportunity deserves only a measured share of your capital.

Flip that around: Prioritize survival. Keep your capital alive and compounding. Find the right balance between safety and growth.

That’s why Kelly works best for repeatable, feedback-rich activities—like investing, running a business, ad campaigns, product testing, or AI projects.

### Common mistakes when applying the Kelly Criterion:

1. **Confusing subjective confidence with objective probability**  
   “I feel 70% sure” rarely equals a real 70% win rate.  
2. **Ignoring correlation between events**  
   Outcomes aren’t always independent—bets can rise or fall together.  
3. **Overlooking extreme downside risks**  
   Data leaks, legal liability, or major brand damage can’t be captured by average probabilities alone.  
4. **Using full Kelly without adjustment**  
   Real-world probabilities and payoffs are noisy. Full Kelly tends to be too aggressive—especially in startups and AI work. A quarter-Kelly or half-Kelly is often more robust.  
5. **Treating irreversible decisions as repeatable games**  
   Kelly assumes you can play again, learn, and adjust. It’s generally *not* appropriate for life-or-death health choices, existential company decisions, or major legal commitments.

For businesses and individuals navigating the AI era, the Kelly Criterion offers four essential operating disciplines:  
- Only invest in opportunities with a *positive edge*.  
- The larger the edge, the more you may prudently allocate.  
- The less certain the probability, the greater the discount on allocation.  
- Never go “all-in” on any single opportunity.

## A User Manual for Life

![Codex Image 2026-08-30 11_03_23.png](图片和附件/Codex%20图像%202026年8月30日%2011_03_23.png)

Wan Weigang, in his *100 Lectures on Modern Thinking* on the GetIt platform, introduced this thought experiment:

*Let’s play a thought game. Imagine you’re a highly accomplished pillar of society—perhaps a major entrepreneur, a prominent social activist, a renowned scientist, or a senior official. You’re now elderly, nearing the end of your life. The good news? You’ll be reborn—and you’ll be reborn in China. The bad news? Rebirth is entirely random: you’ll most likely land in an ordinary family, maybe even a poor one. You don’t want to live as just another ordinary person—but the rules forbid you from secretly stashing wealth for your next life, and all your current knowledge and skills will be wiped clean.*

*Now here’s the loophole: you’re allowed to write a “User Manual for Life” for your next-life self. Your parents will hand it to you once you’re old enough to understand it. So—what would you write?*

It’s a fascinating thought experiment.

I gave it to Codex and asked GPT 5.6 Pro—guided by its understanding of me—to respond. It produced a long, rich *User Manual for Life*, structured around three core goals, seven kinds of assets, and five long-term mechanisms—culminating in the question: *What kind of person should you become?*

**Here’s what it wrote:**

“I’d write it as a letter—and also as a reusable life operating system.

**User Manual for Life**

Dear child,

When you read this letter, you’ve already begun to understand the world—while I have fully left it behind.

You won’t remember my wealth, status, knowledge, or honors. I can’t choose your parents, city, innate talents, or era. The only link between us is that I’ve lived a full life—and you are just beginning yours.

So I’m writing down what mattered most—what I wish I’d known earlier, and what I hope you’ll carry forward.

**I. First, remember your life’s overarching goals**

You have three things to accomplish in this life:

1. **Secure choice.**  
   So you can decide where to live, who to work with, what work to do, what to say no to, and how to spend your time.

2. **Create real value.**  
   Find problems others are willing to pay time, money, trust, or opportunity to solve—and keep solving them.

3. **Help others secure choice.**  
   Once you gain capacity, care for your family, support your peers, nurture newcomers, and improve your organization and society.

To refuse ordinariness doesn’t mean becoming famous—or beating others.  
It means: honoring your gifts instead of ignoring them; refusing to blame fate for everything; choosing growth over numbness while you still can.

**II. Your starting point is just an initial parameter**

You might be born into wealth—or into rural poverty. Your parents may be open-minded—or limited. You may be healthy—or managing a chronic condition.

Don’t pride yourself on your start. Don’t shame yourself for it either.

Family, geography, appearance, talent, and luck shape your path—but they don’t predetermine your ending.

You can understand your parents’ limits—and still leave a harmful environment.  
You can appreciate help you receive—and still think for yourself.  
You can acknowledge life’s unfairness—and then find the best possible path within reality.

Don’t waste your life complaining about the hand you were dealt.  
First, see your cards clearly. Then learn the rules. Find allies. Accumulate chips. Wait patiently for your moment.

**III. Over a lifetime, steadily accumulate these seven assets**

1. **Health**  
   Your body is your first and irreplaceable means of production.  
   Protect your teeth, eyes, spine, sleep, and cardiorespiratory health from childhood. Move regularly. Maintain a healthy weight. Minimize ultra-processed foods. Avoid tobacco, alcohol, drugs, and other addictions.  
   Young people often pay for physical overextension years later.  
   You don’t need extreme wellness—just consistency on a few basics: sleep well, move daily, eat normally, get regular checkups, and act early when something’s off.

2. **Cognition**  
   Read widely: history, science, economics, psychology, law, and how societies actually function.  
   Learn to distinguish fact from opinion, stance from emotion. When facing important questions, ask: Who benefits? Who bears the cost? What did they say—and what did they *do*? Where’s the evidence? What else could explain this?  
   Stay open to revising your views. The most dangerous moment isn’t ignorance—it’s believing you’ve figured everything out.

3. **Skills**  
   No matter how the world changes, master these fundamentals: clear verbal expression, precise writing, math and logic, using the most powerful computational tools of your time, understanding business, grasping human nature, and organizing others to complete complex tasks.  
   On top of that, build at least one marketable professional skill.  
   The most competitive people rarely win by being *best* at one thing—they combine abilities uniquely: tech + business, research + communication, product + sales, expertise + leadership.  
   Being #1 in one area is hard. Being the only one who bridges two valuable domains is rarer—and more valuable.

4. **Work**  
   Don’t just collect knowledge—leave tangible, verifiable, shareable results.  
   Write essays. Run projects. Solve real problems. Ship products. Build case studies. Gather data. Develop methods.  
   Resumes can be polished. Real work is hard to fake. When you lack connections, pedigree, or prestige, your work is your strongest credential.

5. **Trustworthiness**  
   For those who start with little, trust is the earliest intangible asset you *can* build.  
   Keep promises—or explain early if you can’t. Resist petty gains. Arrive on time. Guard confidences. Admit mistakes instead of covering them up.  
   Long-term reliability makes others entrust bigger responsibilities to you.  
   Skill determines whether you *can* deliver. Trust determines whether anyone *lets* you try.

6. **Relationships**  
   Cherish kind, honest, action-oriented people.  
   Distance yourself from chronic complainers, habitual liars, emotionally volatile people, those with no boundaries, and those obsessed with shortcuts.  
   Judge people less by how they describe themselves—and more by how they treat service workers, vulnerable people, opponents in conflict, and failure itself.  
   Never trade dignity for access to a circle. Valuable relationships grow from shared experience, mutual aid, complementary strengths, and long-term trust.

7. **Capital**  
   Money matters. It buys time, education, healthcare, security—and choice.  
   First learn to earn. Then learn to save. Then learn to invest and allocate resources wisely.  
   Don’t chase luxury early. When you’re young, the best things to buy are health, knowledge, tools, mobility, great peers, and capabilities that lift your long-term income.  
   Selling your time is fine at first—but aim to own work, brands, IP, equity, systems, or other assets that generate ongoing value.

**IV. If you’re born into poverty**

Upgrade yourself strictly in this order:

1. Ensure personal safety and basic health.  
2. Seize the lowest-cost, fairest education or selection pathway available in your era.  
3. Master a skill you can sell across regions.  
4. Early exposure to higher-quality information, mentors, peers, and organizations.  
5. Move toward cities, industries, or platforms with denser opportunity.  
6. Build stable cash flow *before* chasing grander ambitions.  
7. Save at least six months of living expenses—so you can walk away from dangerous jobs or toxic relationships.  
8. Shift gradually: from passive execution → independent responsibility → collaborative organization → owning assets instead of selling labor.

Poverty’s deepest danger isn’t just lack of money—it’s how it shrinks your time horizon, forcing you to solve only today’s problems.  
So once you earn your first steady income, consciously rebuild your capacity for long-term thinking.

**V. Choose your environment over proving yourself in the wrong one**

Your surroundings quietly reshape your standards, vision, language, habits, and ambition.  
Seek places dense with capable people, fast-moving information, clear rules, and abundant opportunity.  

Before joining any organization, observe three things:  
- What does it reward?  
- Whom does it promote?  
- What are its best people *actually doing*?  

If flattery, blame-shifting, fraud, or internal sabotage are consistently rewarded—leave. Don’t waste your youth proving you can stay clean in mud.

Be even more careful choosing partners, spouses, and long-term colleagues. Many life gaps stem from just a few deep, lasting commitments.

**VI. Make time your ally**

Nearly everything truly important compounds:  
- Physical fitness compounds through consistent training.  
- Skill compounds through sustained practice.  
- Trust compounds through repeated reliability.  
- Wealth compounds through long-term accumulation.  
- Relationships deepen through enduring presence.  
- Influence grows through persistent output.  

Don’t switch long-term goals constantly—and don’t abandon sound directions just because short-term results are slow.

Ask yourself these seven questions regularly:

- Is my body healthier?  
- Are my judgments sharper?  
- Are my skills rarer?  
- Have I created meaningful work?  
- Do others trust me more?  
- Is my income structure more resilient?  
- Have my choices expanded?

If most are improving—you’re on the right track.

**VII. Choose problems worth solving**

Don’t just ask: *What do I enjoy?*  
Also ask: *What does society genuinely need? Who will pay for it? Will this problem persist? Can I outperform most people at solving it?*  
And after solving it once—can you turn it into a method, product, or system?

Early career: leverage strong platforms and mentors to learn fast.  
Mid-career: develop your own judgment, methods, and work.  
Late career: build systems, train people, allocate resources.  
Never settle permanently in easily replaceable execution roles.  
Keep moving upstream:  
- From accepting tasks → to defining them.  
- From completing work → to designing systems.  
- From selling time → to creating assets.  
- From individual skill → to organizational capability.

**VIII. Understand five mechanisms that shape destiny**

- **Compounding**: Small daily gains are underestimated. So are small daily losses.  
- **Leverage**: Tools, media, code, capital, branding, and organizations multiply individual impact. Learn to use them—while keeping responsibility for outcomes.  
- **Feedback**: Test ideas in reality quickly—not debate them endlessly in your head. Real-world feedback beats imagination every time.  
- **Probability**: Don’t bet everything on one chance, person, company, or call. Increase high-quality attempts—so luck finds you more easily.  
- **Boundaries**: Anything that could permanently disqualify you demands extreme caution. Drugs, gambling, crime, reckless debt, dangerous driving, public fraud, chronic health neglect, or deep entanglement with malicious people can erase years of progress in one move. Life forgives many small errors—but not one catastrophic one.

**IX. Manage your attention**

What you attend to shapes your thoughts, speech, emotions, and character.  
Don’t give your clearest hours to endless feeds—or let algorithms dictate your daily focus.  
Entertainment helps you rest—but prolonged immersion erodes your ability to act.  
Reserve uninterrupted blocks for reading, reflection, writing, training, and doing what truly matters.  
When everyone chases trends, staying deeply engaged with one important problem is itself rare and powerful.

**X. Understand money, fame, and power correctly**

Don’t shy from money—but don’t measure everything by it.  
Money solves many problems—and creates new ones. As wealth grows, so do desires, relationship dynamics, and risks.

Fame amplifies. It magnifies your work—and your missteps. Don’t manufacture a false self just to be seen.

Power means influencing others’ lives. With power comes special need for restraint. Character reveals itself most clearly when no one’s watching—and no one needs pleasing.

Remember always: titles expire, applause fades, wealth fluctuates. What people ultimately recall is *how you treated them*.

**XI. Choose partners and family deliberately**

When selecting a life partner, prioritize values, emotional stability, responsibility, growth mindset, and conflict-resolution style.  
Looks change. Income shifts. Passion cools. Lasting partnership rests on respect, trust, cooperation, and shared burden.

Don’t expect your partner to fill all your loneliness—or try to control another autonomous person.

Once you have children, don’t project your unfinished life onto them. Give them love, safety, boundaries, exposure, and choice—then let them become who they are, not who you hoped to be.

Jobs can be remade. Some relational wounds never fully heal.

**XII. Keep ambition—and keep kindness**

You can strive for achievement, wealth, and larger stages.  
But never look down on ordinary people.

Farmers, factory workers, teachers, drivers, nurses, cleaners—and countless others living quietly—hold society together. True stature doesn’t require belittling the majority.

Your success includes timing, institutions, help from others, and sheer luck. Acknowledging that doesn’t diminish your effort—it grounds you.

When you gain resources, dedicate some to lifting others from where you began. Helping others cross the same chasms you crossed is among the most meaningful uses of wealth and ability.

**XIII. Different ages, different tasks**

- **Childhood**: Protect curiosity. Strengthen your body. Build reading habits. Master foundational subjects. Learn chores. Cultivate discipline.  
- **Young adulthood**: Break out of narrow information bubbles. Seek excellent mentors and peers. Deliver real projects. Build transferable skills. Identify a direction worth deepening.  
- **Around age 30**: Reduce frequent pivots. Solidify professional advantage. Secure stable cash flow. Choose partners and collaborators carefully. Begin acquiring long-term assets.  
- **Middle age**: Shift from personal success to systemic contribution—train people, build organizations, shoulder broader responsibility—while protecting health and family.  
- **Later years**: Gradually release control. Pass on experience. Mend regrets. Curate your work. Serve society. Face the end with calm.

Each stage has its work. Don’t show off when you should be learning. Don’t overthink when you should be acting. Don’t cling to power when it’s time to step back.

**XIV. At major decisions, follow this sequence**

Confirm facts → Analyze interests and costs → List at least three options → Assess worst-case outcomes → Design a low-cost test → Observe feedback → Adjust quickly.  
Once direction is clear—commit fully.

Don’t demand perfect judgment every time. What matters is: winning big when right, surviving when wrong, and correcting faster than others.

**XV. Final words**

You don’t need to walk the same path I did—or become who I was.

I left you no secret fortune—only these principles.

Please strive to become someone who is:  
physically healthy, mentally clear, reliably capable, deeply trustworthy, useful to others, and fully responsible for yourself.

When you lack choice—fight for it.  
When you gain choice—use it wisely.  
When you hold abundant choice—help others claim theirs.

You may still fail. You may still suffer injury. You may lose people you love. You may face injustices you cannot explain. Don’t let that make you doubt life itself.

To have loved deeply, created steadily, borne responsibility, helped others—and lived honestly as yourself—that alone gives a life weight.

And when you too grow old, preparing your own letter for the next life—I hope you’ll add, at the end of this manual, a few new answers I never grasped.”
